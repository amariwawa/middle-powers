A Mutual Gain Proposal · Middle Powers Innovation 2026

No single middle power can test frontier AI alone. Together, they can.

A pact among four to six countries to pool resources for independent, third-party evaluation of frontier models, with a shared registry that ensures who funded a test, and what was disclosed.

The problem

Frontier AI models are expensive, and the labs that build the models decide who tests them. Middle powers are still choosing between building costly national bodies or relying on someone else's results.

Too costly alone

A national eval lab can cost tens of millions yearly. Compute, talent, and red-team access all carry costs that scale rapidly, and few countries can sustain them alone.

Too dependent on two blocs

Relying on US or UK-led AI safety institutes means trusting a potentially biased party. Third-party autonomy erodes as geopolitical pressures shift.

Too little leverage

On their own, mid-sized economies lack bargaining power vis-à-vis concentrated labs or superpowers. Collective voice gets results, fragmented voices don't.

How the pact works

01

Pool the cost

Member states set the evaluation agenda and contribute proportional funding. A lean secretariat manages contracts, logistics, and the shared cost envelope.

02

Condition market access

Using a single agreement, members require frontier providers to submit models for evaluation before their models can reach local markets. Coordination makes compliance more practical.

03

Test, then disclose

Independent evaluators run agreed test suites—safety, bias, factual accuracy, resilience—at a timeline tied to model release. Members set the terms, not labs.

04

Publish the registry

A shared registry records every evaluation result, its methodology, the source of funding, its reproducibility status, and the evaluators. Transparency drives accountability.

Hard questions, direct answers

Why would a lab cooperate?

A credible, multilateral evaluation framework offers labs a single compliance pathway across multiple markets. It replaces fragmented, country-by-country audits with one trusted process.

Why not rely on the US or UK institutions?

Independence matters. The safety institutes in these countries serve national policy interests. A multilateral pact gives middle powers control over the scope, pace, and priorities of testing.

What about free-riding?

The pact is structured so that participation is tied to benefit. Non-contributing countries don't receive full results. Member status carries both cost obligations and access rights.

What the major power objection?

If successful, the pact is another venue for testing that does not displace other evaluations. Laps are already at liberty to pursue independent testing arrangements. A pact formalises something already starting to happen.

The first step is one convening, not a treaty.

One country invites three others to a table to draft a mutual recognition framework — a CONVENING, not a CONVENTION. Agree on the test suites, the cost splits, and the registry. Then, test the idea.
